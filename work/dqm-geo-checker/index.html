Case study

Designing a tool for a field nobody understood yet

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) is about making websites readable for AI agents such as ChatGPT, the way SEO does for search engines. When it emerged, Digital Quality Manager, Crownpeak’s tool for scanning and improving websites, wanted to be first with guidance for it. My job was to turn a high-level concept into practical designs fast, and to check with users that they needed it before large-scale development began. The feature came out of a one-week hackathon and was sold as a separate feature.

A field nobody understood yet

DQM regularly scans clients’ websites and tests their content for SEO, accessibility and usability. As AI agents spread, clients began to need the same kind of guidance for them, and the company wanted to be at the frontier before competitors were.

The catch was that very little was known about GEO, inside the company or out. I held sessions with experts from different teams and disciplines to pool what we knew. While the technical teams learned how the new AI agents actually worked, I focused on what users needed. Time was the main constraint, so I scaled down parts of the research to fit.

Users couldn’t say what they needed

I interviewed users to learn whether they needed this at all, whether they would pay for it, and what information they wanted. This proved challenging: most were still too unfamiliar with GEO to know whether they needed a tool for it.

Borrow from SEO, don’t reinvent

The more we learned, the more GEO looked like SEO. That pointed to the key design decision: present the new concept with the components, terminology and interactions users already knew from DQM’s SEO reporting and from other SEO tools, rather than invent a new interface. Users already had to learn a new skill; they shouldn’t have to learn a new interface as well.

Three days to a working prototype

The company chose the project for a hackathon, a sign of how much it mattered. With a small team, we spent one week turning our findings into a concrete concept. To get something in front of users quickly, I used Lovable, an AI prototyping tool that was new to me, and had a fully functional prototype in three days. It was never meant for deployment; it was meant for early feedback.

What the AI got wrong

The prototype made a lot of assumptions and choices that didn’t fit our product. We kept what it was good for, a first suggestion of what the page should contain, and rebuilt the rest: we rearranged the information, grouped the issues into general categories so users could find what was relevant to them, and applied DQM’s standards and design system. By the end of the hackathon week, we had a realistic-looking design.

Reuse over invention

DQM already had a complete component library, so the GEO page reused the layout and structure of the existing SEO feature. That kept the experience consistent and let us set up a new page quickly. We only built new components where they served a new purpose.

Sold on its own

We chose to launch as soon as possible and validate afterwards. That meant releasing while GEO was still new and relevant, and to users who were more forgiving of small bugs in a brand-new feature.

After launch I interviewed two groups: long-time DQM users now using the feature, and customers who bought DQM just for GEO, a group larger than we’d expected. The interviews confirmed that the core use cases were covered and set the priorities for what to build next.

The feature delivered on both fronts: it shipped out of a hackathon, and it became a commercial success, sold as a separate feature and bringing new customers to the whole platform. [Dummy: 40 new customers in the first 3 months, a quarter of them buying DQM for GEO alone.]
