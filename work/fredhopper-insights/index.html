Case study

Rebuilding a decade-old analytics tool that users didn’t trust

Fredhopper Insights is the analytics platform inside Fredhopper, the product discovery engine behind search, recommendations and filtering for some of Europe’s largest e-commerce platforms. It hadn’t changed significantly in over ten years, and clients didn’t rely on it. I interviewed users and non-users, ran workshops with teams across the company, and designed and validated the new platform, which launched to existing customers in summer 2025.

A tool nobody relied on

Insights should have been where clients checked how their search and merchandising performed. In practice, outdated technology and usability standards had piled up for a decade. The business goal was clear: find where the tool could deliver more value through features, data and usability, and raise adoption, and with it profitability.

The harder constraint was internal. Engineering wanted stability and efficiency; sales and management wanted new features to sell. Before designing anything, I brought all stakeholders in to share what they already knew. That surfaced the conflict early, challenged many assumed technical limitations (a lot of them were overcome), and tested our starting assumptions against data the teams already had.

Users didn’t trust the numbers

I interviewed customers who used Insights and customers who didn’t. Three findings reshaped the brief:

  • The data looked wrong even when it wasn’t. Terminology and calculations differed from industry standards, so numbers didn’t match tools like Google Analytics, and users concluded the data was unreliable.
  • The data wasn’t actionable. Most of it was too generic to tell anyone what to change.
  • There was no reason to use it. Insights offered little data that competing platforms didn’t, so clients had no incentive to open it.

I shared these in workshops with stakeholders from several disciplines, where we mapped the customer journey and prioritised the issues together.

Familiar patterns over new ones

Before solving these issues, I researched how established tools solve them and what the industry standards are, to avoid reinventing the wheel. One decision followed directly from the interviews: we aligned the terminology and calculations in Insights with industry standards, rather than keeping Fredhopper’s own definitions, so its numbers match what users see in tools like Google Analytics.

I sketched on paper first, so feedback stayed at the level of concepts and didn’t jump to details too early. A wireframe of the main pages and how they connect then gave engineering and management something concrete to discuss navigation and content against. The final product moved away from it in places.

Numbers need context

Late in design and during development, edge cases kept appearing. The clearest was the A/B-test overview: a list of significance and improvement percentages meant little on its own. I iterated on the card to make it readable, give quicker access to the relevant details, and add a tooltip that explains each figure.

A component library from scratch

There was no component library for Insights, and the style of the main Fredhopper platform, Merchandiser Studio, had to be considered. As the designs settled, I built one: every component documented in all its states, with colours, typography and iconography, built from reusable parts (atomic design), and tokens named in the same terms as the engineering teams’ code. I also prototyped transitions and motion in Figma, so engineers could see how things should appear rather than guess.

I was also responsible for setting up the design-system standards across the company: see Fredhopper design system.

Most of Insights should stay out of the way and let the data speak. Empty, error and warning states are the exception, and they’re where I put the most personality. They’re the moments people feel the most: doubt when a report comes back empty, frustration when data won’t load, worry when a warning appears. An illustration and a sentence that sounds like a person can soften that blow, or turn an empty first screen into something to look forward to. In Insights, these states use illustrations from one consistent set, next to plain words about what happened and what to try next.

Launched as an MVP, then refined

Together with sales and management, we chose to launch an MVP and decide what came next based on feedback and data, rather than wait for a complete product. The new Insights launched to existing customers in summer 2025. After launch, I interviewed users and the colleagues closest to them, and later customers who still weren’t using it, to find what was missing.
